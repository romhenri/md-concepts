# Supply and Demand

## Definition
Supply and demand is the model describing how the price and quantity of a good are jointly determined by the willingness of producers to sell (supply) and of buyers to purchase (demand) at each possible price.

## Key Idea
Price is not set by either side alone. It settles at the point where the quantity buyers want to purchase exactly equals the quantity sellers want to offer - the market equilibrium.

## Details

### Structure
- **Demand Curve** - slopes downward; as price falls, quantity demanded rises, all else equal.
- **Supply Curve** - slopes upward; as price rises, quantity supplied rises, all else equal.
- **[[market-equilibrium|Market Equilibrium]]** - the price/quantity pair where the two curves intersect; at any other price, a surplus or shortage pushes price back toward it.

### Shifts vs. Movements
- **Movement along a curve** - caused by a change in the good's own price.
- **Shift of a curve** - caused by a change in anything else: income, tastes, input costs, technology, expectations, or the price of related goods.

### Elasticity
- **[[elasticity|Elasticity]]** measures how sharply quantity demanded or supplied responds to a price change, determining whether a shift in one curve mostly moves price or mostly moves quantity.

## Significance
- The basic mechanism by which decentralized markets allocate scarce goods without central coordination.
- The supply side rests on [[opportunity-cost|Opportunity Cost]], since committing resources to produce one good means forgoing whatever else those resources could have produced.
- Both demand-pull (demand outpacing supply) and cost-push (supply costs rising faster than demand) are standard mechanisms behind [[inflation|Inflation]].
- The [[laffer-curve|Laffer Curve]] is a specific application: it traces how the supply of taxable economic activity responds to the after-tax price of working.

## Limitations

### Criticisms
- **[[market-failure|Market Failure]]** names the conditions - externalities, public goods, monopoly power, information asymmetry - under which the equilibrium price no longer reflects the full social cost or benefit of the good.
- **[[externality|Externality]]** is the clearest case: when a cost or benefit falls on a third party outside the transaction, the market price omits it and the "efficient" equilibrium misallocates resources.
- Assumes many small buyers and sellers with no individual power to move price; this breaks down under monopoly, monopsony, or oligopoly.
- Assumes rational, well-informed actors, which behavioral economics and real-world evidence frequently contradict.

## Related Concepts
- [[market-equilibrium|Market Equilibrium]] is the specific outcome the model predicts, and the reference point against which any surplus or shortage is measured.
- [[elasticity|Elasticity]] governs how much of a shift lands on price versus quantity.
- [[opportunity-cost|Opportunity Cost]] underlies the supply curve, since every unit produced represents resources not used elsewhere.
- [[market-failure|Market Failure]] describes when the equilibrium this model predicts stops being efficient.
- [[externality|Externality]] is the specific mechanism by which an unpriced cost or benefit causes market failure.
- [[inflation|Inflation]] arises in part from aggregate demand and supply moving out of step at the economy-wide level.
- [[laffer-curve|Laffer Curve]] applies the supply side of this model to the market for taxable labor and activity.
