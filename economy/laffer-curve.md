# Laffer Curve

## Definition
The Laffer Curve illustrates the relationship between tax rates and the total tax revenue collected by governments. It suggests that tax revenue is zero at both 0% and 100% tax rates, and that there exists an optimal tax rate somewhere in between that maximizes revenue.

## Key Idea
- At **0% tax rate**: no revenue is collected (nothing is taxed).
- At **100% tax rate**: no revenue is collected either, because there is no incentive to work or produce (all income would be confiscated).
- Between these extremes, revenue rises, peaks, and then falls as rates increase - implying that raising tax rates beyond a certain point can reduce total revenue.

## Details

### Origin
Popularized by economist Arthur Laffer in the 1970s, sketched informally to illustrate the trade-off between tax rates and taxable economic activity.

## Significance

### Implications
- Cutting tax rates from very high levels *could* increase revenue by encouraging more economic activity.
- Used to justify supply-side tax cut policies.
- The exact shape and peak of the curve are theoretical and hard to measure empirically - the "optimal" rate varies by economy, tax type, and behavior.

## Limitations

### Criticisms
- Difficult to determine where an economy sits on the curve.
- Oversimplifies taxpayer behavior and revenue dynamics.
- Effects vary significantly across different taxes (income, capital gains, corporate) and time horizons.

## Related Concepts
- The Laffer Curve is a foundation for [[supply-side-economics|Supply-side Economics]], which advocates cutting tax rates to spur growth.
- It is often invoked in debates over [[fiscal-policy|Fiscal Policy]], since it directly concerns government revenue and spending capacity.
- It relates to the [[deadweight-loss|Deadweight Loss]] concept in economics, as excessive taxation reduces economic activity and efficiency.
