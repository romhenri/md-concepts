# Inflation

## Definition
Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level of goods and services in an economy over time, resulting in a fall in the purchasing power of each unit of currency.

## Key Idea
Inflation is a property of the price level as a whole, not any single price. A good becoming more expensive is a relative price change; inflation is when the average of all prices rises persistently.

## Details

### Mechanisms
- **Demand-pull** - aggregate demand grows faster than the economy's capacity to supply goods and services, bidding prices up.
- **Cost-push** - input costs (wages, energy, raw materials) rise and are passed through to consumer prices.
- **Monetary** - growth in the [[money-supply|Money Supply]] that outpaces growth in real output, per the quantity theory of money.
- **Expectations-driven** - if actors expect inflation, they raise prices and wages preemptively, which can make the expectation self-fulfilling.

### Measurement
- Tracked via price indices (e.g. Consumer Price Index) that weight a representative basket of goods and services.
- **Real vs. Nominal** - nominal values are stated in current currency; real values are adjusted for inflation to allow comparison across time, most notably when deflating [[gdp|GDP]] to isolate actual output growth from price growth.

### Policy Response
- **[[monetary-policy|Monetary Policy]]** - the central bank's primary lever, typically raising the [[interest-rate|Interest Rate]] to cool demand and slow price growth.
- **Phillips Curve** - the posited short-run tradeoff between inflation and unemployment, used (and contested) as a guide for how aggressively to tighten policy.

## Significance
- Erodes the real value of savings, fixed incomes, and long-term debt contracts, redistributing wealth between borrowers and lenders.
- Central to the mandate of most central banks, which typically target a low, stable inflation rate rather than zero.
- The [[interest-rate|Interest Rate]] actors care about is the real rate (nominal minus expected inflation, per the Fisher relation), since that determines actual purchasing power gained or lost by lending or borrowing.
- Arises from the same [[supply-and-demand|Supply and Demand]] mechanism operating at the economy-wide level: demand-pull and cost-push are just aggregate demand and aggregate supply moving out of step.

## Limitations

### Criticisms
- Hard to measure precisely - the basket of goods in a price index cannot capture every household's actual consumption or quality changes over time.
- The Phillips Curve tradeoff has proven unstable across decades, undermining it as a reliable policy guide (notably during 1970s stagflation, where inflation and unemployment rose together).
- Very low or negative inflation (deflation) carries its own risks - delayed spending, rising real debt burdens - so "lower is better" does not hold without limit.
- Monetary tightening to fight inflation typically slows growth and raises unemployment, making the policy response costly even when it succeeds.

## Related Concepts
- [[supply-and-demand|Supply and Demand]] supplies both principal mechanisms - demand-pull and cost-push - behind sustained price rises.
- [[monetary-policy|Monetary Policy]] is the primary tool used to bring inflation back toward target.
- [[money-supply|Money Supply]] provides the monetary-channel explanation, where its growth outpacing real output translates into price growth.
- [[interest-rate|Interest Rate]] is both the instrument used to manage inflation and the value that must be adjusted for it to yield a real return.
- [[gdp|GDP]] must be deflated by an inflation measure to distinguish real output growth from mere price growth.
