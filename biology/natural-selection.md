# Natural Selection

## Definition
Natural selection is the process by which organisms with traits better suited to their environment tend to survive and reproduce more successfully than those without such traits, causing those traits to become more common in the population over generations.

## Key Idea
Proposed by Charles Darwin, natural selection acts on existing heritable variation within a population - it does not create variation itself, but determines which variants persist based on their effect on survival and reproduction ("fitness").

## Details

### Requirements
- **Variation** - individuals in a population must differ in traits.
- **Heredity** - traits must be heritable, passed from parents to offspring.
- **Differential Fitness** - some trait variants must confer a survival or reproductive advantage.
- **Time** - change accumulates over many generations.

### Types
- **Directional Selection** - favors one extreme of a trait.
- **Stabilizing Selection** - favors intermediate traits, reducing variation.
- **Disruptive Selection** - favors both extremes over the intermediate.
- **[[sexual-selection|Sexual Selection]]** - a special case driven by mate choice or competition for mates.

## Significance
- Central mechanism driving [[natural-evolution|Natural Evolution]].
- Explains adaptation - why organisms are well suited to their environments.
- Applied outside biology in fields like economics, computer science, and cultural evolution as a general optimization metaphor.

## Related Concepts
- [[mutation|Mutation]] supplies the genetic variation on which natural selection operates.
- [[natural-evolution|Natural Evolution]] is the broader process that natural selection drives.
- [[genetic-diversity|Genetic Diversity]] influences how much material is available for selection to act on.
